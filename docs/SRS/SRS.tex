  \subsection{Purpose}

  \plt{State the purpose of the system.}

  \plt{The purpose should be in the context of the project itself, not in the
  context of this course. Although the ``purpose'' of the document is to get a
  grade, you should not mention this.  Instead, ``fake it'' as if this is a real
  project.}

  The purpose of \projname{} is to make capstone project proposals, discovery, team formation, and
  team project approval transparent, consistent, and easy to manage for the students, supervisors, and
  course instructors involved.

  \plt{What problem does your program solve?
  The description here should be in the problem space, not the solution space.}

  Capstone project and team selection currently happens across disconnected tools such as email,
  online forms, discussion forums, and shared files, leading to a lack of visibility, clarity, and detail in
  information available to students, supervisors, and instructors. As a result, project descriptions do not set clear
  expectations, students and supervisors cannot easily tell which projects are available or learn about each other, and
  instructors spend significant time manually coordinating the process.
  See \nameref{sec:problem-statement} for a more detailed description of the problem.

  \projname{} is intended to solve these problems by giving every participant a single, trustworthy
  source of information about capstone projects, teams, and their status, from the moment a project
  is proposed until it is approved.

\textbf{Problem being solved.} The fragmented and unreliable flow of information in capstone
  project and team selection and approval, as described in the \nameref{sec:problem-statement} section below.

  \textbf{Intended users.} Capstone students, project supervisors (McMaster faculty and industry
  sponsors), course instructors, and teaching assistants (TAs), across the Software Engineering (SE),
  Computer Science (CS), and Mechatronics (TRON) capstone courses.

  \textbf{Major functionality.} \projname{} covers the lifecycle from project proposal to team project approval.
  Within that boundary the system:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item provides proposal templates for supervisors and for students, so that projects are described
    consistently;
    \item lets instructors review supervisors' project proposals before they are listed, and agree changes
    with the supervisors;
    \item provides a searchable catalog of projects showing which are open, taken, or able to take
    more teams;
    \item lets students and teams create structured profiles of their skills, experience, interests, and work style
    that other students and supervisors can view, indicate whether they are looking for a team,
    and find teammates interested in the same projects;
    \item suggests compatible teammates and suitable projects to students, based on their profiles and preferences
    \item lets student teams express interest in supervisor-proposed projects;
    \item supports communication between all users regarding project proposals, team formations, applications and
    and approvals, ensuring discussions are organized and accessible to relevant parties;
    \item supports comment threads on projects, student posts, and applications, so that questions and
    answers are kept in one place;
    \item lets supervisors and student teams each accept or decline working together, and records when both
    have agreed;
    \item notifies users of events that need their attention, such as new projects, status changes, and replies;
    \item provides supervisors with course process, timeline, and expectation information, and examples of
    past capstone projects, configurable by the course instructor;
    \item gives instructors visibility across courses into projects, teams, and their status, including
    which supervisors' projects were not selected, so that those supervisors can be informed;
    \item provides guidance and automation to help students meet all requirements when submitting their projects for
    approval, and when submitting changes to their project information;
    \item lets instructors approve, request changes to, or reject a team's project submission and later
    changes to its information;
    \item lets instructors configure which features are enabled and disabled for full flexibility
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{What \projname{} does not do.}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item It does not judge whether a project is feasible, sufficiently complex, or appropriate
    for a capstone course; this remains the responsibility of the course instructor.
    \item It does not make approval decisions. Final approval of teams and projects always rests
    with the course instructor. Courses may carry out approval inside the platform or in an external process.
    \item It does not resolve intellectual property (IP), confidentiality, or non-disclosure
    questions between students and supervisors.
    \item It does not find supervisors for student-proposed projects. Student-proposed projects are
    visible to all users of the platform, but \projname{} provides no matching or request mechanism
    for supervisors.
    \item It does not schedule meetings or provide direct messaging between users. Meetings take place
    outside the platform, and discussion is limited to comment threads on posts and applications.
    \item It does not handle supervision after a project begins, such as feedback requests to
    supervisors, team performance concerns, or grading.
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Expected inputs.} Inputs are text-based project and profile information entered by users,
  and project status changes made by authorized users.

  \textbf{Expected benefits.} Students can make more informed decisions on which projects to pursue,
  and find compatible teammates; supervisors know who is interested in their projects,
  learn about the students, and receive fewer repeated emails; and instructors spend less time on repetitive
  coordination and gain visibility into the whole process.

  \subsection{Problem Statement}

  \label{sec:problem-statement}

  \plt{Current situation, problem, impact, desired improvement.  You will likely borrow some text from your problem description document.}

  \textbf{Current situation.} Capstone team and project selection is spread across disconnected
  tools, including email, online forms, MS Teams, Avenue to Learn and GitLab.
  Supervisors submit proposals through a form. The SE course instructor, who collects proposals for all
  three courses, reviews them, discusses any changes with the supervisor by email, and edits the list by hand
  before it is distributed to students as a static list.
  Students then form teams informally, mainly through discussion
  forums on Avenue to Learn / Teams and personal contacts, contact supervisors by email, and
  submit their chosen project and team for instructor approval through a course-specific process.
  No single place shows the state of the whole process.

  \textbf{Problems.} The following problems were identified through interviews with stakeholders and general observation.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Project descriptions are inconsistent and do not set expectations.} Proposals vary
    widely in the level of detail, and very short descriptions put students off. Some of the things
    students want to know include a project's end goal and expected impact. Also, descriptions often do not
    state how much of the project already exists. Supervisors are unsure what a capstone project
    should look like and what a proposal should include, and students who pitch their
    own ideas do not know what a proposal needs. This makes projects difficult to compare and leads
    to mismatched expectations.
    \item \textbf{Student-proposed projects are hard to see.} Students' own project ideas appear only in
    discussion forums, where supervisors cannot see them, including supervisors who would like to
    supervise a student-proposed project. They are not tracked alongside supervisor-proposed projects.
    \item \textbf{There is no visibility of project status.} Students and supervisors cannot see which
    projects are taken or open, or whether a supervisor is willing to take more than one team, and after a
    rejection students do not know which projects remain. New projects and updates are hard to notice, and
    users do not want to have to remember to check for them.
    \item \textbf{Compatible teammates are hard to find.} Students without an existing network rely on
    forum posts or chance. Teammates' project preferences, skills, and work styles are often unknown until
    a team has formed, so teams can disagree about what to build.
    \item \textbf{Supervisors do not know the students' backgrounds.} Supervisors have little structured
    information about the students and teams who contact them, such as their skills, experience, and
    program. They can only gain this information through interviews or rely on outside sources such as
    LinkedIn profiles, which are inconsistent and not always available.
    \item \textbf{Communication during selection is inefficient.} Supervisors receive repeated emails
    from teams, including after a team has been chosen. What one team learns about a project in a meeting
    is not shared with other teams applying for the same project. Nobody can see which teams are in contact with
    which supervisors. There is also no record that a team and a supervisor agreed to work together.
    Students sometimes report that a supervisor agreed when the supervisor has no record of the team, so
    the instructor must confirm agreements separately.
    \item \textbf{Supervisors lack guidance before proposing and responding.} Before proposing a project,
    supervisors receive only a short summary of the course's expectations and process. The full course
    information arrives only after teams and projects are settled, and supervisors want it earlier, along
    with examples of past projects. Supervisors are also unsure how quickly to reply to students and
    whether to speak to every team that contacts them. This leads to misunderstandings about what a
    capstone project should deliver and to many questions to the instructor.
    \item \textbf{Instructors lack visibility across courses.} Instructors cannot see which projects
    students in other capstone courses selected, so they cannot tell which proposed projects were not
    selected. Supervisors whose projects are not selected are not informed, which may discourage them from
    proposing projects in future years.
    \item \textbf{Instructor review is repetitive and time consuming.} Instructors review each team's submission manually and often
    give the same comments to many teams on simple items, such as missing information, wasting the instructor's time.
    When reviewing project proposals, changes to proposals that are agreed with supervisors by email must then be
    applied to the project list by hand.
    \item \textbf{Records drift after approval.} Team and project information changes after approval (for
    example, titles, supervisors, links, team composition, and TA assignments), but updating the official
    record takes extra steps, so it is often not updated and the instructor's records become out of date.
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Impact.} Students lose time early in a project of limited duration, teams end up
  mismatched with their projects or with each other, supervisors may become reluctant to propose
  projects in later years, and course instructors carry the administrative burden of the process.

  \textbf{Desired improvement.} All participants share a single, accurate and current source of
  information about projects, supervisors' capacity, teams, and approval status; students can find
  suitable projects and teammates; supervisors can see students’ skills and ideas and receive
  information about the course when they need it; and course instructors keep full control of
  approval decisions while spending less time on repetitive tasks.

  \subsection{Product Vision}

  \plt{A clear and concise statement of the product's purpose and goals.}

  \plt{The goal statements are functional and nonfunctional objectives that system
  under consideration should achieve. Goals provide criteria for sufficient
  completeness of a requirements specification and for requirements pertinence.
  Goals will be refined by requirements.  The goals are written abstractly, with
  a minimal amount of technical language.  They should be understandable by
  non-domain experts.}

  \plt{You will likely borrow from your goal statements in your problem
  description document.}


  \projname{} will be the central place where capstone projects are proposed, discovered, and
  matched with student teams, and where instructors can see and manage the process. Each course
  decides which features it uses and how its approval process is carried out, so the platform can
  be adopted by different courses, departments, and universities.

  The goals of \projname{} are:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Consistent project information.} Every project, whether proposed by a supervisor or by
    students, is described in a consistent format that makes its objectives, expected impact, required
    skills, current state, and expected outcomes clear.
    \item \textbf{Visibility of availability and updates.} Users can tell which projects are open, taken, or
    able to take additional teams, and are made aware of new projects and changes without having to check
    for them.
    \item \textbf{Easier team formation.} Students can describe their skills, experience, work style, and
    project preferences in a structured profile, find teammates with compatible profiles and preferences,
    and propose projects of their own for others to join.
    \item \textbf{Informed matching.} Students receive suggestions of compatible teammates and suitable
    projects based on their profiles and preferences.
    \item \textbf{Informed supervisors.} Supervisors can see student-proposed projects and the skills and
    experience of the students and teams interested in their projects.
    \item \textbf{Efficient communication during selection.} Questions and answers about a project or
    application are kept in one place, supervisors can see which teams are interested in their projects,
    and fewer repeated emails are needed.
    \item \textbf{Clear agreement between supervisors and teams.} A supervisor's and a team's agreement to
    work together is recorded in the platform and is visible to the instructor.
    \item \textbf{Timely guidance for supervisors.} Supervisors have access to information about the
    capstone course, its timeline, and its expectations, and to examples of past projects, when they
    consider proposing a project, as configured by the course instructor.
    \item \textbf{Oversight for instructors.} Instructors can see projects, teams, supervisors, and their
    status across courses, including projects that were not selected.
    \item \textbf{Instructor-controlled approval with less repetitive work.} Final approval decisions remain
    with the course instructor. The platform supports the project proposal approval process, guiding supervisors to write
    more detailed project proposals and facilitating discussion between the instructor and supervisor.
    The platform supports the team project approval process carried out in the course,
    whether that takes place in the platform or elsewhere, helps students submit complete and correct
    information, and helps keep records current.
    \item \textbf{Configurable and reusable.} Features can be turned on or off, and course-specific
    rules can be configured, so that the platform serves different courses without modification.
  \end{enumerate}

    No information about students before team meetings; a leap of faith was
    required, relying on gut feeling and the right questions. Did not know
    they had the final say.
  \item[ES\refstepcounter{esn}\theesn \label{ES_StudentProfiles}]
    Wants a way to learn about students (team bio, interests, skills,
    experience), even if not all shown up front; looking them up (e.g.,
    LinkedIn) was mentioned.
  \item[ES\refstepcounter{esn}\theesn \label{ES_Matching}]
    Wants teams matched to projects based on preference criteria and skill
    sets.
  \item[ES\refstepcounter{esn}\theesn \label{ES_WhoToEmail}]
    Hard to find where to communicate back to students. Groups usually had one
    representative email and CC their members, which made emails hard to
    search. With many names and no group numbers, they defaulted to the first
    person who emailed. Inviting everyone was too taxing; wants some way to
    streamline.
  \item[ES\refstepcounter{esn}\theesn \label{ES_Admin}]
    Overall, there is a lot of administration on the supervisor side.
\end{itemize}

% ==================== INTERNAL SUPERVISOR ====================
\paragraph{Internal Supervisor (IS)} \label{app:stk_intsup}
Professor at the university who supervises teams, submits proposals, and
advises the instructors.

\noindent \begin{itemize}
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Role}]
    Mostly a supervisor. Submits proposals and then waits to see what else is
    on the list in case something interests him. Interviews and supervises
    groups, attends lectures when possible, helps with presentations and Expo
    posters, and is often on the outside of the course.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_StudentProposed}]
    Unsure how student-proposed projects will be handled.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_ApprovalTiming}]
    The three capstone courses are not well coordinated. The timing and method
    of the approval process (whether a project can be a capstone project) is
    not really known.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Standardize}]
    Current descriptions are 100--200 words. Standardizing them is hard and he
    is unsure what the standard should be. Some supervisors may want something
    specific, and one group delivered something related to, but very different
    from, the proposal.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Schedule}]
    Supervisors do not know the course schedule (when the SRS is due, when
    implementation starts, validation testing, user testing). There is
    confusion about what happens and when things are due.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_MultiCourse}]
    Supervisors with teams across multiple courses (SE/CS/Tron) may not know
    that each course has different timelines and requirements.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Procedure}]
    No standardized procedure, e.g., how a group with an idea finds a
    supervisor. Wants the timeline and procedure centralized and standardized.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Reports}]
    Wants a low-effort mechanism for supervisors to submit a report or
    feedback at the end. One professor sends feedback emails, but others do
    not seem to remember them and send reports voluntarily.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Communication}]
    Communication and regular updates (check-ins) between supervisors and
    teams are essential and need to be accounted for. Discussion is needed on
    weekly meetings and who questions go to (TA or professor).
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_KnowTA}]
    Wants to know who each of his groups' TAs are.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_StudentSkills}]
    Student skills would help guide discussions with supervisors.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Scope}]
    Would like the capstone process all in one place, but is unsure whether
    that would work with other professors, or whether the platform should
    cover only the proposal stage.
  \item[IS\refstepcounter{isn}\theisn \label{IS_Accounts}]
    Accounts are mentioned as a need (details not captured in notes).
\end{itemize}

% ==================== Software Engineering Instructor ====================
\paragraph{Software Engineering Instructor (SEI)} \label{app:stk_instr}
Professor who runs the course.

\noindent \begin{itemize}
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_CurrentProcess}]
    Current process: before the course, emails contacts to find projects.
    Interested supervisors submit an MS Form (title, organization, contact,
    description, confidentiality, IP concerns). A Google Form funnels into a
    spreadsheet that a script turns into LaTeX. Students form teams, choose
    projects, and organize meetings. A merge request in GitLab counts as
    approval.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_SupervisorChanges}]
    Supervisor details often change and are updated. Supervisors are contacted
    to confirm they know a team will work with them, which often surfaces
    Expo and other capstone details.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn]
    Teams are classified by project hardware (SE vs.\ Mech).
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_TeamJSON}]
    Team details are recorded in a JSON file, processed and verified by a
    Python script (e.g., every person is in exactly one team). The team
    composition CSV should be an output that is up to date, correct, and valid.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Decisions}]
    Would rather spend time on decisions such as project viability and
    approval.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Recruiting}]
    Often has to reach out to potential supervisors to have enough projects
    each year.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Unselected}]
    Wants to know which projects have not been selected, e.g., to send a thank
    you email.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Automate}]
    Coordination, communication, and details such as approved projects and
    groups should be automated as much as possible.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_ApprovalCriteria}]
    Approval criterion: enough substance. Game projects often need extra work
    to add substance (e.g., more analysis such as proving fairness, or more
    components).
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Standards}]
    Standards could be considered, but enforcing them can scare away good
    projects; optional questions may help.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_NoAIApproval}]
    No AI agents making approvals. AI-generated summaries could be considered,
    but their usefulness is unclear.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_PRs}]
    Pull requests are still favored because they expose students to CI and
    show its usefulness, and allow back-and-forth between professor and
    students.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_RepeatFeedback}]
    The same feedback is sent to multiple groups. An AI agent could respond to
    PRs with already-resolved issues (e.g., professor not added as
    collaborator), leaving more important work to the instructor.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_Universal}]
    Possibly usable universally, which may require modularity and
    extensibility.
  \item[SEI\refstepcounter{instn}\theinstn \label{I_MatchingOptional}]
    Algorithmic matching of teams to projects could be interesting, with a way
    to turn it off. Overhead must be worth it; this could be offset by moving
    other things (e.g., meetings) from Teams or Zoom onto this platform.
\end{itemize}
