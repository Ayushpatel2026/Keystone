\subsection{Problem}

\wss{What problem are you trying to solve?  Why is it important? Do not talk
about how you will solve the problem, only what the problem is.  AI is rarely
part of the problem.  The problem is ``what'' you want to do, not ``how'' you
want to do it.  The problem statement should be written in a way that is
understandable to a non-technical audience.}

Capstone team and project selection at McMaster is currently fragmented
across multiple disconnected tools, including Teams, GitLab, MS Forms, and
email. This fragmentation creates administrative overhead, frustration, and
a lack of transparency for everyone involved. Beyond the fragmentation
itself, the information within these tools is often unreliable.

Project descriptions vary widely in accuracy and level of detail, making it
difficult for students to compare opportunities and understand what a
project actually involves. Due to this lack of clarity, students can lose
valuable time early in the process, which can be critical given the limited
duration of an eight-month project. Supervisors, in turn, may become
reluctant to offer projects in future years if expectations are
consistently miscommunicated, reducing the range of opportunities available
to future cohorts.

Furthermore, students have limited visibility into which projects are
still available, and there is no unified way to track a project's status
from initial submission through final team assignment.

Beyond finding a project, students also struggle to find teammates who
share similar skills, work styles, and interest in the same projects. Team
formation currently happens informally, through personal networks or
chance, rather than through any structured process.

Together, this lack of transparency, unreliable information, and absence of
structured team formation lead to wasted time, mismatched teams, and added
administrative overhead for course instructors and TAs.


\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}

\wss{Characterize the problem in terms of ``high level'' inputs and outputs.  
Use abstraction so that you can avoid details.}

\textbf{Inputs:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Project proposals and descriptions submitted by supervisors
  \item Student profiles
  \item Student expressions of interests and applications to projects
  \item Administrative decisions (approvals, status changes, etc.)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Outputs:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A searchable and filterable catalog of available projects
  \item Project status and availability information
  \item Confirmed project--team assignments
  \item Suggested teammates based on student profiles
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Stakeholders}


\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Capstone Students} --- The primary users of the platform.
  They browse and filter available projects, express interest or apply, and
  form teams. Their main need is clear, accurate, and up-to-date
  information so they can make informed decisions and avoid wasted effort
  pursuing unavailable or misrepresented projects.

  \item \textbf{Capstone Project Supervisors} --- Professors or industry
  sponsors who submit and manage project proposals. Their main need is a
  simple, standardized way to describe their project, specify how many
  teams they are willing to take on, and manage student interest without
  relying on ad hoc email communication.

  \item \textbf{Capstone Course Instructor} --- Oversees the capstone
  process as a whole. Needs visibility into which projects and teams exist,
  the ability to track progress across the cohort, and administrative
  control to resolve issues

  \item \textbf{Capstone Course TAs} --- Support the coordinator in
  administering the process. Need efficient tools to help manage
  day-to-day tasks such as verifying project submissions, tracking
  applications, and assisting students and supervisors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Goals}

The primary goal of the platform is to provide a centralized and structured system for managing the capstone project process. The platform will improve the experience for students, academic supervisors, industry sponsors, and administrators by making project information easier to access, manage, and coordinate.

\subsection{Core Goals}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Centralized Capstone Project Management:}
    Provide a single platform where capstone projects can be submitted, viewed, managed, and tracked throughout their lifecycle.

    \item \textbf{Project Discovery:}
    Allow students to search and filter available projects based on factors such as skills, technologies, project type, industry, supervisor, and availability.

    \item \textbf{Student and Stakeholder Profiles:}
    Allow students, academic supervisors, and industry sponsors to create profiles containing relevant information such as skills, interests, experience, and project preferences.

    \item \textbf{Team Formation:}
    Allow students to indicate their intent to form or join a team, and provide visibility into other students seeking teammates for the same or similar projects.

    \item \textbf{Project Interest and Application Management:}
    Provide students with the ability to express interest in projects and allow supervisors as well as sponsors to review and manage interested students.

    \item \textbf{Standardized Project Submissions:}
    Provide a consistent project submission format that ensures important information, such as objectives, requirements, required skills, and expected deliverables, is clearly presented.

    \item \textbf{Administrative Management:}
    Provide administrators with tools to manage users, projects, project availability, and project assignments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Stretch Goals}

The following features may be implemented if sufficient development time and resources are available.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Team Compatibility Matching:}
    Recommend potential teammates with respect to their skills, interests, and project preferences.

    \item \textbf{Intelligent Project Recommendations:}
    Create a recommendation system that makes suggestions and evaluates compatibility between projects and students based on the student's skills, interests, and preferences.

    \item \textbf{Analytics Dashboard:}
    Provide administrators with statistics regarding project demand, student engagement, applications, and project availability.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Ayush Patel:}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What went well while writing this deliverable?

    It was really easy for us to come up with problems and goals because this is something we've actually experienced as a team.
    Our initial project fell apart because the supervisor's expectations didn't match ours, and on top of that we had a hard time figuring out which projects were still open.
    That first-hand experience made it really easy to write the problem, goals, and stakeholder sections since we weren't just guessing at pain points, we lived them.

    Given the nature of our project, it was also pretty simple to figure out which custom documents we needed.
    We'll definitely need to interview a bunch of people (students, supervisors, instructors) to nail down requirements, and we'll also need usability testing to make sure we actually built something usable by all three groups.

    \item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
    did you resolve them?

    The main pain point was figuring out how to keep our goals abstract enough that they didn't just turn into requirements in disguise.
    We kept catching ourselves writing things like "the system shall do X," which is way too detailed for a goal.
    We resolved this by going back and forth as a team, asking "is this describing a capability, or is this describing how it works," and rewording anything that leaned too far into implementation.

    \item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure
    they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
    appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?

    In our initial discussion with our TA, it came up that our scope might be a bit too small for a capstone project.
    But if we added a full team-matchmaking algorithm as core scope, that would make things too big.
    So we split the difference: basic team formation (letting students indicate they want a team and see who else is looking) became a core goal, and the smarter, algorithm-driven matchmaking became a stretch goal instead.

\end{enumerate}
